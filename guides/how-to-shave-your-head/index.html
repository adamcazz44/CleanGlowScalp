The shave

How to shave your head: a complete routine

The whole thing, start to finish — prep, the right tool, technique that avoids nicks and bumps, how to handle the back you can't see, and the aftercare that keeps your scalp healthy.

Updated July 20, 2026

The part nobody warns you about is the back of your own head. You can't see it, and the first few times you'll miss a patch or leave it uneven. The fix is mostly technique and a second mirror — here's the routine I wish I'd had on day one.

First, pick your tool

There's no single right answer — it's a trade-off between closeness and convenience.

  • Manual razor. The closest, smoothest finish. It also takes the most care, the most time, and carries the most nick risk — especially on the parts you can't see.
  • Electric head shaver. Faster and far more forgiving on blind spots, and it leaves a hair of stubble, which tends to mean fewer bumps. If a multi-blade razor leaves you irritated, dropping to a simpler razor or an electric shaver often helps.

We compare specific head shavers in a separate guide (linked below). Whatever you use, keep the blade sharp — a dull blade tugs and causes nicks and bumps.[source]

The routine, step by step

1. Soften the hair

Shave at the end of a shower, or hold a warm, damp cloth on your scalp for a few minutes first. Warm, wet hair is softer and swells, so it cuts cleanly instead of tugging — which means fewer bumps.[source]

2. Lather up (for a wet shave)

Spread a moisturizing shave cream or gel over the whole scalp and give it a minute to work. Keep it wet while you shave — if it dries out, rinse and re-apply. (Shaving dry with an electric shaver? Skip this step.)

3. Shave with the grain, lightly

Go in the direction the hair grows, not against it — against the grain is closer but irritates more and drives hairs back into the skin.[source] Use light pressure and short, overlapping strokes, and don't stretch the skin tight. A good pattern: front to back across the top first, then the sides, overlapping each pass so you don't leave stripes. Rinse the blade often, and try not to go over the same spot more than twice.

4. Handle the back you can't see

This is the part that takes practice. Set up a two-mirror system — a mirror on the wall behind you and a handheld one in front — so you can actually see the back of your head. Then trust your free hand: run your palm over the scalp to feel for stubble you missed, and work the blind areas slowly in overlapping passes. An electric shaver is much more forgiving here than a blade.

5. Rinse, cool, and check

Rinse all the product off with warm water — leftover cream irritates skin. A cool, damp cloth afterward calms things down. Then do a final pass with your palm and the mirrors to catch any missed patch before you dry off.

6. Aftercare

Pat dry and finish with a soothing, alcohol-free aftershave balm or a light scalp moisturizer — a balanced scalp is less prone to flaking and excess shine. And if you're heading outside, sunscreen on the scalp is non-negotiable; a bald head burns and collects sun damage fast.

The common mistakes

  • A dull blade — the single biggest cause of nicks and bumps.
  • Shaving against the grain chasing a closer cut.
  • Too much pressure, or stretching the skin taut.
  • Going over the same patch again and again to "get it perfect."
  • Skipping sunscreen afterward.

If it keeps going wrong

A few nicks while you learn is normal. But if you're getting persistent bumps, ingrown hairs, or irritation no matter how you shave, that's its own thing to solve — start with our razor bumps and ingrown hairs guide, and see a dermatologist if it won't settle. Common questions

Electric head shaver or a manual razor?

Both work; it's a trade-off. A manual razor gives the closest, smoothest finish but takes more care and time and carries more nick risk. An electric head shaver is faster, far more forgiving on the parts you can't see, and leaves a hair of stubble that means fewer bumps. Most guys who shave daily land on electric for convenience.

How do I shave the back of my head I can't see?

Two things: a two-mirror setup (a wall mirror behind you plus a handheld in front so you can see the back), and your free hand. Most of the back gets done by feel — run your palm over the scalp to find stubble, and work in slow, overlapping passes. An electric shaver makes the blind areas much more forgiving.

Wet or dry?

A wet shave (in or after a shower, with cream or gel) gives the closest result and softens the hair first. A dry electric shave is faster and more convenient. Many head-shavers do a wet shave a couple times a week and quick dry touch-ups in between.

How often should I shave my head?

Whatever keeps the look you want — daily for fully smooth, every two to three days if you don't mind light stubble. Shaving a touch less close (or leaving slight stubble) tends to mean fewer razor bumps and less glare, so there's no prize for the closest possible shave.
