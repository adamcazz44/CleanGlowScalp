Sun spots on a shaved head: prevent and fade them

The flat brown marks that creep across a bald scalp aren't just age — they're sun. Here's what they are, how to stop new ones, what actually fades the ones you've got, and the line where a spot becomes a job for a doctor.

Updated July 20, 2026

I shave my head, and I didn't think much about the top of it until a few flat brown spots showed up where the sun hits hardest. Turns out that's the most predictable thing in the world for a bald scalp — and most of it is preventable. This is what I wish I'd known earlier.

What sun spots actually are

The marks people call sun spots, age spots or liver spots have a real name: solar lentigines. They're flat, well-defined brown patches caused by years of ultraviolet light pushing your skin to make and hold extra pigment.[source] They're benign, and they're extremely common with age — found in up to 90% of fair-skinned people over 60.fair skin, 60+ [source]

Here's the catch that surprises people: unlike freckles, which fade in winter, solar lentigines don't pack up and leave. Once they form, they tend to stay. That's why the real win is stopping new ones rather than chasing the ones you have.

Why a shaved head is a hotspot

A full head of hair is a built-in sun shade. Shave it off and the scalp takes direct overhead sun all day with nothing in the way. That's not a small detail: skin cancer of the scalp is more common in men than women, largely because of male-pattern baldness leaving that skin exposed.[source] The scalp and hairline are also some of the most commonly missed spots when people apply sunscreen.[source]

Sun spots themselves are harmless. But they're a visible receipt for the UV exposure that also drives the things that aren't — which is the whole reason prevention is worth taking seriously.

How to stop new ones (this is the real fix)

Fading existing spots is slow. Preventing new ones is fast, cheap, and the thing dermatologists put first. Four habits do almost all the work:

  • Daily broad-spectrum SPF on the scalp. Every day you're outside, not just beach days. A dedicated scalp or face sunscreen that doesn't run into your eyes or leave a heavy white cast makes this easy to actually keep up. Reapply every two hours when you're out in it.[source]
  • A hat when you'll be out a while. A wide brim shades the exact areas that catch the most sun. It's the simplest backup to sunscreen.
  • Shade during peak hours. UV is strongest roughly 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. Stepping under cover during that window cuts a lot of exposure.[source]
  • Take sunburns seriously. They stack up. Your lifetime risk of melanoma roughly doubles after more than five sunburns.lifetime sunburns [source]

How to fade the ones you already have

First, a non-negotiable: have a dermatologist look before you treat a spot yourself. Some skin cancers look like ordinary sun spots, and if you treat a cancer as a cosmetic blemish you just give it time to grow. A quick skin check rules that out — and caught early, skin cancer is highly treatable.[source]

Once you know a spot is just a spot, you've got two routes:

At home, with patience

Over-the-counter products can fade pigment gradually if you use them daily for weeks to months. Dermatology sources point to ingredients like vitamin C, niacinamide, glycolic acid, kojic acid, and retinoids, with hydroquinone as a stronger lightening agent.[source] None of it works without sun protection — skip the SPF and you undo the progress as fast as you make it.

In a dermatologist's office, faster

Procedures work quicker than creams but cost more and carry more risk of side effects. The common ones for sun spots are freezing (cryotherapy), laser or intense pulsed light, and chemical peels.[source] A dermatologist can also tell you whether a prescription cream is the better call for your skin.[source]

When it's not just a sun spot

Most brown marks on a scalp are harmless. A few aren't, and the top of your head is hard to see — so it's worth knowing the signals. See a dermatologist if a spot:

  • changes in size, shape, or color over weeks or months;
  • has uneven color or a ragged, blurred border;
  • itches, bleeds, scabs, or won't heal;
  • feels rough and scaly, like sandpaper (this can be a precancerous patch called an actinic keratosis, which can turn into the most common scalp skin cancer if left).[source]

None of this is a diagnosis, and it isn't a reason to panic — it's a reason to get a quick check. More on where we draw that line is on our not-medical-advice page. Not sure if a change is worth a check? See scalp warning signs.

Common questions

Do sun spots on a bald head go away on their own?

Generally no. Unlike freckles, which can fade in winter, true sun spots (solar lentigines) come from years of built-up sun exposure and tend to stay put once they form. You can fade them with treatment, but the reliable move is preventing new ones with daily sun protection.

Does sunscreen on the scalp really matter if I'm not burning?

Yes. The damage that creates sun spots adds up quietly over years, well below the level of a visible burn. A shaved scalp takes direct overhead sun with no hair to shade it, so daily broad-spectrum SPF is the single most effective thing you can do.

Can I get rid of sun spots at home, or do I need a dermatologist?

Mild spots often fade with consistent at-home care over weeks to months. In-office procedures work faster. But see a dermatologist before you start treating a spot yourself — some skin cancers look like harmless sun spots, and treating one as a blemish just delays a diagnosis.

What's the difference between a sun spot and something to worry about?

A typical sun spot is flat, evenly colored, and has a clear border, and it doesn't change much. Get any spot checked that is changing in size, shape or color, has uneven color or a ragged border, itches, bleeds, or feels rough and scaly like sandpaper.
